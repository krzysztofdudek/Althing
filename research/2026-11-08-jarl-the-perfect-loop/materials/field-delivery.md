# Review, merge, build and release: what the best engineering organisations do

Written on 2026-11-08 by a research agent (Opus 5.5 at xhigh) from public sources, primary where it could open them; where it relied on a summary, the sources say so. Kept as it reported.

## 1. Principles

**P1. The main line only moves to a state that passed the full check on the exact merged result** (Graydon Hoare's "not rocket science rule": "automatically maintain a repository that never fails its tests"). Mechanism: a branch green on its own can turn red once merged; Uber's iOS main line was green only 52% of the time before it had a queue, and Google's book says such collisions happen "most days at our scale". For agents: only the merger's recorded check on the merged result can mark an issue done, never the worker's or the reviewer's word; "branches touch no common file" is a weak proxy for independence. Limit: done strictly in series, a check of m minutes over n changes takes m×n; Jane Street saw changes wait up to 24 hours behind builds of two hours or more.

**P2. Size batches from the measured failure rate, and let the size adapt.** Mechanism: a batch of n units that each fail with probability p is green with probability (1−p)^n. Zuul's window starts at 20, grows by 1 after each merge and halves after each failure; Rust's bors marks each change at approval as rollup `always`, `maybe`, `iffy` or `never`. For agents: record the queue's red rate per class of unit; batch prose and other low-risk work widely; keep units that touch shared inputs (lock files, CI configuration, contracts, shared code) alone; shrink the batch after a red. Rule of thumb (standard group-testing arithmetic, not from these sources): the best batch is about 1/√p, so about 2 at p = 0.25, 4 to 5 at 0.05 and 10 at 0.01. Limit: a flaky red sends the bisect after an innocent unit.

**P3. Test speculatively in parallel, in order of predicted success.** Mechanism: Zuul, GitLab merge trains and the GitHub merge queue test each entry on top of those ahead of it; a red removes only the failing entry and restarts those behind. Uber prunes its speculation tree with a predictor (97% accurate) whose strongest signals are tests passed before submission, earlier failed speculations and the number of resubmissions. For agents: when the check is long and machines are free, check the batch tip and its prefixes at once, so a red already shows which unit broke it; the loop already holds the predictor's inputs (review rounds, Important findings, earlier reds of that worker or model, the size of the diff). Limit: compute; before Uber's 2025 fix, 40 to 65% of builds were aborted early. Speculation pays only when the check is slow compared with the merge decision.

**P4. Two changes conflict when they share build or test targets, not when they share files.** Mechanism: Uber's conflict analysis compares hashed build targets; Bazel's hermetic actions and Nx "affected" choose tasks by declared inputs. For agents: work out each branch's affected set from the repository's build or import graph, and use it both for what may be batched together and for which tests the worker runs. Limit: undeclared inputs and outputs silently miss dependents, and lock files and other shared inputs affect everything, so the affected set is advice and the full check stays the gate.

**P5. Before handing over, a worker runs the fast, reliable checks for what it touched; the full check runs in the queue; the queue's measured red rate decides where the line sits.** Mechanism: Google's presubmit runs "only fast, reliable" tests and 95% or more of changes that pass it then pass everything else; Meta's predictive selection runs a third of the dependent tests and catches more than 99.9% of regressions. For agents the economics differ: workers are parallel and cheap, the queue is serial, and every red there costs a bisect plus reruns of every unit behind. Always run the affected tests, lint and types before handover; run the whole check before handover when the change touches widely used inputs, when its class shows a high red rate in the queue, or when the check takes minutes rather than hours. A worker's statement that it ran something is not evidence; evidence is output the merger can run again. Limit: dozens of agents running the full check compete for one machine, and a green branch is still not a green merge.

**P6. The unit of review is one self-contained change, and size is the strongest lever on what a review finds.** Mechanism: Google's guide calls 100 lines "usually reasonable" and 1,000 "usually too large"; SmartBear says defect finding falls beyond 400 lines; Bosu and colleagues found the useful share of comments falls as the number of files rises. On Jarl's own 400-line split: an Important or Critical finding in about half the large units probably means they carry more defects and that the review catches a smaller share of them; it does not show review works better on large units. For agents: keep packages, which workers gain from, but cap the reviewed diff at about 400 changed lines (not counting generated code and deletions); land one commit per issue so the reviewer reads issue by issue, with evidence per issue; for a change made by a tool, review the tool plus a sample of its output, as Google does with large-scale changes. Limit: too fine a split costs coordination (Google's least satisfied authors had changes of one or two lines); human fatigue numbers (60 minutes, 500 lines an hour) do not carry over to models as they stand, so measure them.

**P7. Keep three attestations apart: correctness, ownership and fit, conventions.** Mechanism: Google asks an approval for each ("LGTM", owner, readability), usually from one person holding all three; in Kubernetes `/lgtm` comes from any reviewer and `/approve` from an approver in the nearest OWNERS file; in GitHub's CODEOWNERS any one listed owner suffices, and the CODEOWNERS file should own itself. For agents: the reviewer agent gives the correctness approval; areas whose rules are the owner's (architecture, contracts, suppressions, public wording) need his approval, mapped by path as OWNERS files map it, with the mapping file protected the same way; the counterpart of readability is a model and brief trusted for a class of task on their measured record, not on a claim. Limit: the owner becomes the bottleneck; Google's answer for large changes is global approvers and automatic approval of output that matches the expected pattern, so humans see only the exceptions.

**P8. Catch each defect at the cheapest stage, and when one gets through, move its detection earlier.** Mechanism: a bug's cost "grows almost exponentially the later it is caught"; Google's static analysis (Tricorder, 110 analyzers) shows in review with a "Not useful" button that prunes noisy analyzers; matklad's ratchet adds a check with a list of existing violations that may only shrink. For agents: much of review's value at Google and Microsoft is teaching and shared norms, which stateless agents learn only if written into the repository, so every recurring finding and every queue red becomes a candidate check or a line in a brief. Limit: too many checks, and noisy ones nobody trusts; measure each check's false-alarm rate.

**P9. A flaky test is a defect in the check, dealt with before the check may gate.** Mechanism: at Google 1.5% of test runs are flaky and 84% of pass-to-fail transitions involve a flaky test; the large-change system ignores recently flaky tests; Zuul keeps a window per project so one project's flakes do not slow the others; hermetic tests stay deterministic. For agents: before bisecting a red batch, rerun the failing test on the batch tip; quarantine flaky tests as issues of their own; a worker that retries until green has produced a false green. Limit: quarantine can hide a real regression, a risk Google takes knowingly.

**P10. Fast turnaround by one competent reviewer beats more reviewers, guarded against rubber stamps.** Mechanism: Google has a median of one reviewer and a median review under 4 hours, against 15 to 24 hours elsewhere; Meta targets the 75th percentile of time in review and adds "Eyeball Time" as a guard against rubber stamps. For agents: start the reviewer as soon as the branch is ready; require it to cite file and line and to rerun at least one evidence command; measure its recall by sometimes planting a known defect (the agent's proposal, not an established practice). A second reviewer of another model for `never`-class units and for units over 400 lines. 

**P11. Keep branches short-lived and integrate in levels as the work grows.** Mechanism: trunk-based development allows about two days, review wait included, and warns that many worktrees become long-lived branches "spread sideways" and that "the unit is one developer and their coding agents"; Jane Street's hierarchical release cut central traffic from hundreds of requests a week to 30 to 40; Zuul runs shared queues and `Depends-On:` links across repositories. For agents: worktree branches merge within hours; a release's feature branch is itself long-lived, so sync it often with its repository's main; each repository's queue batches its own units, then a workspace checks the release's exact commits together, like a Zuul shared queue. Limit: every level adds latency.

**P12. Release on a train, expose progressively, and freeze when the budget is spent.** Chrome's branch point is fixed and work not ready "should be punted" to the next milestone; canaries run one at a time, judged on a few metrics traceable to the change; an exhausted error budget halts every change but P0 and security fixes, and one incident using more than 20% of the budget requires a postmortem with a P0 action; feature toggles are inventory with a carrying cost, kept in check by expiry dates and tests that fail once a toggle expires. For agents: trains (what is not green on the merged result at the gate waits for the next release); a canary (use the release candidate in a real loop before publishing); an escape budget (defects found after merge; when it is spent, stop merging features and work on checks); postmortems that fix the brief, check or rule, not the agent.

## 2. Numbers

- **Google code review** (Sadowski et al. 2018; logs of 9 million reviewed changes, 12 interviews, 44 survey responses): median 24 lines changed; over 35% of changes touch one file, about 90% fewer than 10, over 10% change a single line; median 1 reviewer, fewer than 25% have more than one; first feedback a median under an hour for small changes and about 5 hours for very large ones; whole review a median under 4 hours, 70% committed within 24 hours of being sent; over 80% need at most one round of fixes; comments per change peak at 12.5 at about 1,250 lines; reviewers spend 3.2 hours a week (median 2.6). For comparison (Rigby and Bird, cited there): median approval 14.7 to 24 hours; median change 44 lines at AMD and 263 at Lucent.
- **SmartBear on a Cisco team** (vendor material; base given in secondary sources as 2,500 reviews of 3.2 million lines over 10 months): 200 to 400 lines a session, below 500 lines an hour, at most 60 minutes, said to find 70 to 90% of defects.
- **McIntosh et al.** (Qt, VTK, ITK): low review coverage goes with up to 2 more post-release defects per component, low participation with up to 5.
- **Google testing:** TAP handles more than 50,000 changes a day and runs more than 4 billion test cases; 95% or more of changes passing presubmit pass everything else; the average wait to submit is about 11 minutes; for large changes a TAP train starts every 3 hours and runs 1,000 sampled tests per change, the full union can take over 6 hours, and large changes are 10 to 20% of a project's changes. Flaky tests (Micco 2016): 1.5% of runs flaky, about 16% of tests show some flakiness, 84% of pass-to-fail transitions involve a flaky test.
- **Uber SubmitQueue:** without a queue the iOS main line was green 52% of the time over one week; about a 40% chance of a problem with 16 concurrent changes (nine months of iOS and Android data); predictor 97% accurate; turnaround within 1.2 times a perfect oracle. The 2025 follow-up: builds per change fell from 5.43 to 1.96 on Android; 95th-percentile waits fell 31 to 45% across three monorepos.
- **Meta:** predictive test selection runs a third of the dependent tests and catches more than 99.9% of regressions; the median wait in review is a few hours but the 75th percentile rose by up to a day; NudgeBot cut average time in review by 7% and the share of changes waiting over 3 days by 12%.
- **Zuul:** the window starts at 20, grows by 1 after each merge, halves after each failure. **GitLab:** a merge train runs 20 pipelines in parallel by default. **Rust:** a CI run takes about 3 hours; a rollup holds about 10 changes for a newcomer (1 iffy, 4 maybe, 5 always) and up to about 20 for an experienced maintainer. **Jane Street:** a full build took two hours or more and a change could wait up to 24 hours.
- **DORA 2024** (estimates, correlation only, read in summaries): higher AI adoption goes with throughput down 1.5% and stability down 7.2%; any batch that takes more than a week to finish and check is too big.
- **SRE canarying:** a 5% canary serving 20% errors shows only 1% errors overall.

## 3. Anti-patterns an agent loop is likely to fall into

Trusting the claim: accepting "tests pass" from a worker, or treating a green branch as a green merge. Treating "no shared files" as proof two changes are independent. Bisecting on a flaky red, or retrying until green. Letting batches or packages grow while the green rate looks fine, as bisect cost and missed defects grow with them. A reviewer of the worker's own model, or a review that cites nothing and runs nothing. Review findings that never become checks, so the next stateless worker repeats the mistake. Jumping the queue for "urgent" fixes (GitHub warns of a full rebuild; GitLab says changes that skip the train are not checked against it). Long-lived release branches drifting from each repository's main; many parallel worktrees spreading sideways. Large generated changes, which AI tools tend to produce. The owner reviewing every shard instead of only the exceptions. Growing toggle debt and allow-lists. Watching the median wait and missing the slow tail.
