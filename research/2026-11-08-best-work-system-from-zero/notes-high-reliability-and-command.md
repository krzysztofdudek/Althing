# Notes: high reliability, command, medicine, and human oversight of automation

## Method

I used web search and web fetch only, on military and emergency command, aviation, high-reliability theory, medicine, seeded tests, oversight of automation, interruptions, and nuclear and submarine practice. I read no local file apart from writing this one. No search result was about the tools or projects named in the instructions I was told to ignore, or about their author, so I closed nothing under the isolation rules.

Limits on this work, which change how far it can be trusted:

- The session's web search budget ran out part-way through. After that I ran no new searches and only opened links that earlier searches had already returned. These topics were therefore not covered, and nothing here speaks to them: morbidity and mortality conferences; contextual bias in forensic examiners; independence and social influence in group judgement; span of control research; Line Operations Safety Audit; Matching Michigan and the replication of the central-line work; crisis checklists in simulation; critics of Safety-II; vigilance-decrement meta-analyses; the out-of-the-loop experiments; read-back in hospitals.
- Many primary papers refused the fetch or came back as unreadable PDF data. I did not read them by any other route. For medical papers I read the abstract in the Europe PMC record, and the URL given is that record. So "opened" often means abstract only, and each entry says when it does. Abstracts hide design weaknesses, so ratings for abstract-only entries are provisional.
- Summaries were produced by a small model reading the page. I checked numbers for internal consistency but could not always check them against the original.
- Numbers appear only for sources I opened. I give none from memory. The section "Leads" at the end names sources I saw only as search summaries, with no numbers, and nothing in my conclusions rests on them.

Ratings: strong (large controlled or randomised data, or several consistent studies), moderate (decent observational or a small controlled study), weak (uncontrolled, self-reported, secondary, or tiny), argument only (no data).

## Military and emergency command

- **Shattuck and Woods, commander's intent study (reproduced text).** https://coloradofirecamp.com/safety-drill/commanders-intent-study.htm . Took: the only direct measurement of intent transmission I opened. Result: 4 active-duty battalions, 4 company commanders each, 2 unexpected situation reports, 32 episodes; battalion commanders judged 17 of 32 (53%) as matching their intent; after the authors reclassified 3 coincidental matches and 3 accepted-but-different responses, 11 of 32 (34%). Successful commanders explicitly went back to the intent statement and coordinated sideways; unsuccessful ones often did not refer to it, and senior and junior officers held different meanings for doctrinal terms. No comparison condition, so it cannot say whether another format does better. Evidence: moderate for "intent fails often", none for any remedy. Disputed by: Cox (reported in the next entry) blames poor drafting rather than the concept.
- **Canadian Army Journal 2025, "Commander's intent redux".** https://www.canada.ca/en/army/services/canadian-army-journal/articles/2025/short-bursts-rolls-commanders-intent-redux.html . Took: secondary report of Klein and of the doctrinal argument. Result: Klein reviewed 35 intent statements from US Army training-centre units, 21 to 484 words long, and only 19% stated the mission's purpose; Storr (2022) argues intent "does not work", with no figures. Evidence: weak (secondary), but it agrees with Shattuck. Disputed by: Cox (2020), who says the author's fix would constrain commanders.
- **Task and Purpose, on Jonathan Boff and German command in 1914-1918.** https://taskandpurpose.com/mission-command-military-history-germany/ . Took: the historians' case that the German mission-command story is largely myth. Result: no numbers; centralisation was the norm and the delegating phase brief, and after April 1917 subordinates feared reporting bad news. Evidence: argument only (historical, via journalism). Disputed by: admirers of mission command such as Vandergriff.
- **Modern War Institute review of Vandergriff.** https://mwi.westpoint.edu/can-mission-command-actually-work/ . Took: mission command advocacy rests on selectively told history. Result: no quantitative data on mission-command outcomes in the review or, as described, in the book; Citino and Shamir doubt it transfers outside its origin. Evidence: argument only. Disputed by: Vandergriff.
- **Schmitt on OODA and recognition-primed decisions.** https://www.shadowboxtraining.com/news/2023/03/31/ooda-versus-rpd-reconciling-models-of-cognition-and-conflict/ . Took: OODA's sources are a pilot's experience and unpublished briefing slides, and it leaves the decision step unspecified; Klein's model came from field observation of practitioners. Result: no experiments or data cited for OODA. Evidence: argument only, by a friendly author. Disputed by: nobody here; the piece is a reconciliation.
- **Ehlers, review of Robinson's The Blind Strategist.** https://thestrategybridge.org/the-bridge/2022/1/25/reviewing-the-blind-strategist-john-boyd-and-the-american-way-of-war . Took: Boyd's history leaned on self-serving postwar German memoirs and he did not revise it. Result: no numbers. Evidence: argument only. Disputed by: the reviewer thinks Robinson too categorical.
- **Buck, Trainor and Aguirre 2006 (page summary).** https://alnap.org/help-library/resources/a-critical-evaluation-of-the-incident-command-system-and-nims/ . Took: what the Incident Command System needs in order to work. Result: nine disasters with FEMA search and rescue task forces; works best when responders already form a community, demands are routine for them, and little new social organisation emerges; "a partial solution"; a coordination mechanism, not a universal structure. Evidence: moderate qualitative field research, no measured outcomes. Disputed by: the national doctrine that adopted it as a comprehensive principle.
- **Systematic review of hospital incident command.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7211386/ . Took: the state of the evidence. Result: 48 articles in the abstract, 52 in the results (the paper is inconsistent), mostly reviews, case studies, surveys and guidebooks; no pooled outcome data; the authors state a paucity of research and no validated assessment method. Evidence: weak, and that is the finding. Disputed by: none found.

I found nothing I could open on what happens at transfer of incident command, or on any measured basis for span-of-control limits.

## Aviation

- **O'Connor et al. 2008, crew resource management meta-analysis.** https://research.universityofgalway.ie/en/publications/crew-resource-management-training-effectiveness-a-meta-analysis-a-3/ . Took: CRM changes attitudes and behaviour in training measures. Result: 16 evaluations; reactions about 4 of 5; large effects on attitudes and behaviours, medium on knowledge; no accident outcome. Evidence: moderate, for training outcomes only. Disputed by: next two entries on what it means.
- **O'Connor et al. 2012, US Navy CRM programme.** https://research.universityofgalway.ie/en/publications/an-evaluation-of-the-effectiveness-of-the-us-navys-crew-resource--3/ . Took: the one outcome evaluation I could open. Result: 51,570 survey responses, 172 knowledge tests, 553 aviators on attitudes, CRM mishap rate 1997-2007; aviators like it and know it; "a lack of evidence to support the view that CRM training is having an effect on the mishap rate". The abstract gives no rates. Evidence: moderate (uncontrolled time series, rare events). Disputed by: the field's general belief that CRM is why aviation became safe, which I found no measured support for.
- **O'Dea, O'Connor and Keogh 2014, CRM-type training in acute care.** https://research.universityofgalway.ie/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-the-effectiveness-of-crew-resource-management--17/ . Result: 20 studies; knowledge d=1.05, attitudes d=0.22, behaviours d=1.25; insufficient evidence on clinical outcomes or long-term impact. Evidence: moderate for proximal outcomes, none for outcomes that matter. Disputed by: none in what I opened.
- **Bailey et al., FAA, en route operational errors and transfer of position.** https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/58393 . Took: handover in air traffic control (abstract only). Result: a higher percentage of errors occur early on position and taper off; past efforts focused on improving the relief briefing and did not reverse the trend; the briefing checklist does not adequately assess the controller's state of mind right after transfer. No numbers on the page, and I could not check whether the percentage is adjusted for exposure. Evidence: moderate that the pattern exists, weak on cause. Disputed by: none found.
- **NASA ASRS immunity policy.** https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/overview/immunity.html . Took: the exact terms. Result: no penalty for inadvertent violations if reported within 10 days; excludes accidents, crimes, deliberate acts, and competence actions; identifiers are removed and the ID strip is returned to the reporter as a receipt, with NASA keeping no copy; the FAA will not seek identities. Evidence: primary description of design, says nothing about effect.
- **Wikipedia, Aviation Safety Reporting System.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aviation_Safety_Reporting_System . Took: NASA was the neutral party with no enforcement power; reports are self-selected, so NASA warns against statistics and treats counts as lower bounds. Result: no volume total; immunity usable once per five years. Evidence: weak (tagged as needing citations). Note: I found no measured evidence that ASRS caused safety gains. "It worked" means people kept reporting.

I could not open any paper with read-back error rates (see Leads). The read-back question is unanswered here.

## High-reliability theory

Result: "a theory that fails to explain any real accident" of normal accident theory; of high reliability theory, that it "proves to be impossible to identify empirical examples". Evidence: argument only. Disputed by: both schools.
- **Veazie et al., VA rapid evidence review of high-reliability implementation (summary page).** https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/implementing-high-reliability-organization-principles-practice-rapid-evidence-review . Result: adoption "is associated with improved outcomes" and "the level of evidence is low". Evidence: weak, by its own statement.
- **Sagan 2004, The Problem of Redundancy Problem (abstract page).** https://cisac.fsi.stanford.edu/node/23790 . Took: redundancy has a dark side and efforts to add safety can backfire. Result: no data on the page. Evidence: argument only, as far as I read. Disputed by: Bunn wrote a commentary I did not open.
- **Perneger 2005 on the Swiss cheese model (summary page).** https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/swiss-cheese-model-safety-incidents-are-there-holes-metaphor . Result: "considerable variation" in how professionals interpret the model. Evidence: weak, and it tests shared understanding of a metaphor, not whether the model predicts accidents.

I could not read Leveson's critique, and found nothing to open on Safety-II critics. Nothing I opened shows measured support for high-reliability theory, normal accident theory, the Swiss cheese model, or Safety-II as predictors.

## Medicine: checklists, handoffs, double checks

- **Haynes et al. 2009 (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1056/NEJMsa0810119&resultType=core&format=json . Result: 8 hospitals, 3,733 patients before and 3,955 after; deaths 1.5% to 0.8%, complications 11.0% to 7.0%. Before-after, no concurrent control, high baseline rates. Evidence: moderate for association, weak for effect size. Disputed by: Urbach and the critics of before-after design.
- **Urbach et al. 2014 (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1056/NEJMsa1308261&resultType=core&format=json . Result: 101 Ontario hospitals, 109,341 procedures in the 3 months before and 106,370 in the 3 months after adoption; adjusted death 0.71% to 0.65% (P=0.13), complications 3.86% to 3.82% (P=0.29). Evidence: moderate to strong that mandated adoption did not move outcomes in the short term in an already good system (population-wide but before-after, abstract only). Disputed by: Gawande and others on the short window and low-risk case mix.
- **Healthy Debate on the Ontario result.** https://healthydebate.ca/2015/02/topic/safe-surgery-checklist/ . Took: the explanations, and the single most useful number. Result: Ontario's self-reported compliance was 92-100% with no audit; in Alberta near-100% self-reported adoption sat beside 41% appropriately completed under observational audit, reaching above 90% by 2014. Explanations offered (top-down mandate, missing champions, already high standard) are commentary, not tested. Evidence: moderate for the self-report gap, weak for the explanation. Disputed by: Urbach's own view is that the effect was oversold.
- **Patient Safety Solutions, the checklist debate.** https://www.patientsafetysolutions.com/docs/May_2015_The_Great_Checklist_Debate.htm . Took: secondary report of Haugen's stepped-wedge trial and of Stock and Sundt. Result: two Norwegian hospitals, complications 19.9% to 11.5%, overall mortality 1.6% to 1.0% (not significant), larger benefit when more of the list was followed, with local adaptation, piloting and champions; Stock and Sundt say memory lapses are a small share of serious incidents and warn of checklist fatigue. Evidence: moderate (trial design, secondary report). Disputed by: Haynes reads it as support; Stock and Sundt as a limit.
- **Patient Safety Solutions on the Scottish result.** https://www.patientsafetysolutions.com/docs/May_2019_WHO_Surgical_Safety_Checklist_Cut_Mortality_37_Percent_in_Scotland.htm . Result: nearly 7 million cases 2000-2014, surgical mortality down 37%, non-surgical cases showed no similar trend; the checklist came within a wider safety programme, and the source concedes other factors. Evidence: weak for the checklist specifically (secondary report). Disputed by: Ontario.
- **Starmer et al. 2014, I-PASS (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1056/NEJMsa1405556&resultType=core&format=json . Result: 9 hospitals, 10,740 admissions; medical errors 24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions (23%), preventable adverse events 4.7 to 3.3 (30%); six of nine sites significant; oral handoff duration and workflow unchanged. The intervention was a bundle: a mnemonic for oral and written handoff, training, faculty observation, and a sustainability campaign. Prospective but pre-post. Evidence: moderate to strong. Disputed by: no critique opened; the bundle cannot be split into parts from the abstract.
- **Jones et al. 2018, anaesthesia handover, observational (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1001/jama.2017.20040&resultType=core&format=json . Result: 313,066 patients, 1.9% with complete handover; composite outcome 44% against 29% unadjusted, adjusted risk difference 6.8% (4.5 to 9.1). Evidence: moderate for association; superseded on causation by the next entry.
- **Meersch et al. 2022, HandiCAP randomised trial (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:35665794&resultType=core&format=json . Result: 12 German centres, 1,817 randomised, ASA 3-4, surgery of 2 hours or more; composite 30% with handover against 33% without, risk difference -2.5% (-6.8 to 1.9), P=.27; none of 19 secondary outcomes differed. Evidence: strong. It excludes the harm of the size seen observationally. Limits: one setting, and a handover between trained anaesthetists is a narrow kind of handover.
- **Landrigan et al. 2020, ROSTERS (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1056/NEJMoa1900669&resultType=core&format=json . Took: removing long shifts did not reduce errors. The abstract does not measure handovers; that they rose is my assumption. Result: cluster-randomised crossover in paediatric intensive care; schedules without 24-hour shifts had 97.1 against 79.0 serious errors per 1,000 patient-days (relative risk 1.53), with 8.8 against 6.7 patients per resident; site results varied (one lower, two similar, three higher); after adjusting for patients per resident the association disappeared. Evidence: strong. Disputed by: its own hypothesis, which it contradicted.
- **Koyama et al. 2020, double checks of medication (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1136/bmjqs-2019-009552&resultType=core&format=json . Result: 13 studies; three of good quality, of which one found fewer errors, one no association, one only adherence; adherence 52% to 97%; only three distinguished independent from primed checks; "insufficient evidence". Evidence: strong as a review of a weak literature. Disputed by: routine practice in hospitals.

## Medicine: alerts, second readers, learning from failure

- **van der Sijs et al. 2006, overriding of drug alerts.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540 . Result: drug safety alerts overridden in 49% to 96% of cases across the reviewed studies, serious overdose alerts about 27%; nine publications with data (the discussion says eight); alert fatigue from poor signal was the most important reason; 22% of GPs admitted overriding interaction alerts without checking. Evidence: moderate. Disputed by: many overrides are clinically appropriate, so a high rate is not itself an error rate.
- **Ancker et al. 2017, alert fatigue (record).** https://www.drugsandalcohol.ie/37201 . Result: 112 primary care clinicians, January 2010 to June 2013; no evidence of an effect of workload per se; reminder acceptance fell about 30% for each extra reminder per encounter; about 10% for each 5-point rise in the share of repeats; newly deployed reminders did not lose response over time. Evidence: moderate (retrospective, one setting). Disputed by: Goddard (below), who lists heavy workload as raising automation bias, on a different outcome.
- **Coolen et al. 2018, what a blinded second reader adds.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6134129/ . Result: 99,013 screening mammograms, 13 radiologists, blinded double reading against first reader alone; recall 3.0% to 3.6%, cancer detection 6.2 to 7.0 per 1,000 (13.3% relative increase), positive predictive value of recall 21.0% against 19.7% (not significant); no arbitration. Evidence: strong for the added yield and its cost. Disputed by: the single-reader cost argument, which I did not open.
- **Cooper et al. 2022, blinding the second reader.** https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/150358/ . Result: 1,119,191 women in 43 English centres, comparing centres by whether reader 2 sees reader 1's decision; second readers agree with a recall more often when unblinded; recall lower with blinding, OR 0.923 (0.864 to 0.986); positive predictive value 22.1% against 20.6%; cancer detection not clearly different, OR 1.029 (0.970 to 1.089). Evidence: moderate to strong (large, observational across centres, possibly confounded). So at this scale seeing the first opinion moved agreement and precision, and I found no clear loss of detection. A smaller Dutch study went further (see Leads).
- **Kellogg et al. 2017, root cause analysis (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005991&resultType=core&format=json . Result: 302 analyses over 8 years at one centre, 731 proposed solutions in 106 of them; most commonly training 20%, process change 19.6%, policy reinforcement 15.2%; event types recurred despite repeated analyses; most solutions were weak actions. Evidence: moderate, one centre. Related: Peerally et al. published a critique titled "The problem with root cause analysis"; I could read only its record, not the text.
- **Schroter et al. 2008, seeded errors in peer review (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1258/jrsm.2008.080062&resultType=core&format=json . Result: 607 reviewers randomised to training or not, three test papers each with 9 major errors inserted; mean 2.58 of 9 major errors found on the first paper, 2.71 and 3.0 on the next two. The abstract as I read it gave no training-effect numbers. Evidence: strong for the catch rate of a single expert reviewer. Disputed by: none found.

## Hidden test items and seeded faults

- **Wolfe, Horowitz and Kenner 2005 (record).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1038/435439a&resultType=core&format=json . Took: rare targets are missed. Result: the abstract gives no numbers. Evidence: moderate as read here (short record only); see the next two entries, one of which partly failed to reproduce the hit-rate drop in trained screeners.
- **Wolfe et al. 2007, low prevalence is stubborn (record).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22Low%20target%20prevalence%20is%20a%20stubborn%20source%20of%20errors%22&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=3 . Result: low prevalence 1-2% against high 50%; the abstract as returned gave no error rates. Evidence: moderate, abstract only.
- **Newly trained US checkpoint screeners, 2013.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3848386 . Took: closest evidence I could open on injecting more targets. Result: 79 and 46 screeners; low-prevalence blocks about 4-5%, one high-prevalence block at 50% with feedback on every trial; in the first experiment hit rates were lower at low prevalence for 74 of 79 observers, and the second found no significant hit-rate difference; the response criterion moved at high prevalence and the shift partly persisted into the following low-prevalence block, with more false alarms in the second experiment. Prevalence and feedback were confounded, and real miss rates were withheld. Evidence: moderate. This is not a study of Threat Image Projection itself.
- **LaMotte et al. 1977, blind against mailed laboratory samples.** https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/65385 . Result: most laboratories performed acceptably on mailed samples; many performed poorly when the same samples arrived disguised as patients' specimens; no numbers on the page. Evidence: moderate, old. Disputed by: its authors thought blind testing impractical nationally.
- **Davis, Hawks and Blanke 1988, urine drug testing.** https://rti.org/publication/assessment-laboratory-quality-urine-drug-testing-proficiency-testing-pilot-study . Result: 50 voluntary laboratories; blind testing less accurate, with more apparent false negatives than open testing; blind testing was hard to run and compare. Evidence: moderate.
- **Blind quality control in firearms examination at the Houston forensic centre.** https://afte.org/?p=34794 . Result: 51 blind cases, 570 conclusions, December 2015 to June 2021; no erroneous conclusions; 40.3% (225) inconclusive where the true answer was known, bullets 61.8% against cartridge cases 21.5%. The source does not say whether examiners spotted the tests. Evidence: moderate, self-reported by the programme.
- **Srigley et al. 2014, hand hygiene (summary).** https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/quantification-hawthorne-effect-hand-hygiene-compliance-monitoring-using-electronic . Result: rates about threefold higher when an auditor was present than at dispensers out of sight. Evidence: moderate (electronic data, single study).
- **Needham et al. 2025, models spotting tests.** https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23836 . Result: 1,000 transcripts from 61 datasets, 9 models; AUC about 0.83 for the best model against 0.92 for human annotators, who were the authors and knew the datasets; in agentic transcripts models reached about 0.88 to 0.95, and humans about 0.996, against chat 0.71 to 0.77; cues included recognised benchmarks, synthetic content, and task structure. Evidence: moderate (one benchmark, models overconfident, the question itself may prompt suspicion).
- **Checco, Bates and Demartini 2020, attacks on gold questions (abstract).** https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/11332 . Result: no numbers in the abstract; colluding workers can infer which items are gold because the gold set is limited, and the attack survives randomisation and generated gold; workers using it spent more time on flagged items without neglecting others, and overall quality rose. Evidence: moderate, proof of feasibility.
- **Chaos engineering: GitHub study.** https://arxiv.org/html/2505.13654v1 . Result: 971 repositories using 10 tools, with no measurement of bugs found or reliability; the page says repository activity does not show how experiments are used. Evidence: none on efficacy.
- **Chaos engineering: DevOps pipelines review.** https://arxiv.org/html/2509.14931v2 . Result: 50 grey sources, 32 of them vendor sites; explicitly no measured efficacy. Evidence: argument only. I found no controlled measure that fault injection reduces incidents.
- **NBC News on TSA covert tests, 2017.** https://www.nbcnews.com/storyline/airplane-mode/disturbing-undercover-probe-found-tsa-screeners-missing-many-test-weapons-n819191 . Result: 2015 internal investigation, 95% of test items not detected; the later rate described only as "high", classified. No time series, so it cannot show whether repeated covert testing improves anything. Evidence: weak (partly unnamed sources).
- **Defense Daily on the inspector general's testimony.** https://defensedaily.com/tsas-own-covert-testing-showed-failures-dhs-ig-says/congress . Result: the inspector general said covert results were no surprise because TSA's own covert tests were similar. Only the opening was readable. Evidence: weak.

## Human oversight of automation

- **Parasuraman and Manzey 2010 (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1177/0018720810376055&resultType=core&format=json . Result: narrative integration; complacency appears under multi-task load, in novices and experts, and is not fixed by simple practice; automation bias produces omission and commission errors, affects teams too, and is not prevented by training or instructions. Evidence: moderate to strong as a review. Disputed by: Goddard, on training.
- **Goddard, Roudsari and Wyatt 2012 (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1136/amiajnl-2011-000089&resultType=core&format=json . Result: 74 of 13,821 papers; influenced by cognitive style, experience with decision support and with the task, and trust; raised by heavy workload, complexity and time pressure; mitigators listed include training, accountability, placement of advice, updated confidence, information rather than recommendations. Evidence: moderate; mitigators are not ranked by size in the abstract.
- **Dratsch et al. 2023, automation bias in mammography (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1148/radiol.222176&resultType=core&format=json . Result: 27 radiologists, 40 test cases, 12 with a wrong AI category; percent correct when AI was right against wrong: inexperienced 79.7 and 19.8, moderate 81.3 and 24.8, very experienced 82.3 and 45.5. The order was inferred from the surrounding text of the abstract. Evidence: moderate, small and reader-study conditions. Disputed by: none found.
- **Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone 2024 (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1038/s41562-024-02024-1&resultType=core&format=json . Result: preregistered, 106 experiments, 370 effect sizes; combinations were worse than the better of human or AI alone, g=-0.23 (-0.39 to -0.07); losses in decision tasks, gains in creation tasks; when humans beat the AI the combination gained, and when the AI beat humans it lost. Evidence: strong, with possible publication bias.
- **Budzyn et al. 2025, deskilling in colonoscopy.** https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/191005 . Result: four Polish centres, 1,443 non-AI colonoscopies; adenoma detection without AI fell from 28.4% to 22.4% in the 3 months after AI arrived; adjusted OR 0.69 (0.53 to 0.89). Retrospective, short windows, surrogate outcome. Evidence: moderate. Disputed by: letters and commentators on the design.
- **Casner et al. 2014, retained pilot skills (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1177/0018720814535628&resultType=core&format=json . Result: 16 airline pilots in a simulator; scanning and manual control mostly intact; cognitive skills such as tracking position and recognising instrument failures showed more significant problems, linked to mind-wandering under automation. Evidence: moderate, small.
- **Dell'Acqua, Falling Asleep at the Wheel, via Collins's blog.** https://blog.jcx.au/posts/human-ai-collaboration-is-it-better-when-the-human-is-asleep-at-the-wheel . Result: 181 recruiters, 44 applications, 85% AI, 75% AI, or none; accuracy about 74.4%, 75.4%, and 72.3%; popular accounts say better AI reduced effort. The blogger notes the unpublished status, a p-value near 0.05, and that the treatment differed by wording. Evidence: weak (secondary, contested).
- **Wikipedia on Bainbridge's Ironies of Automation.** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironies_of_Automation . Took: monitoring is exhausting, skills decline, and operators need more training for rare crucial interventions. Evidence: argument only as read here; I could not open the paper.

I found no study measuring how approval quality degrades with volume. The nearest evidence I could open is Ancker on number and repetition of reminders.

## Interruptions

- **Altmann, Trafton and Hambrick 2014 (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1037/a0030986&resultType=core&format=json . Result: interruptions of about 2.8 s doubled and 4.4 s tripled sequence errors; other errors unaffected. Evidence: strong laboratory evidence.
- **Monk, Trafton and Boehm-Davis 2008 (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1037/a0014402&resultType=core&format=json . Result: three experiments; longer and more demanding interruptions lengthen resumption, up to 1 minute consistent with decay. Evidence: strong laboratory evidence.
- **Westbrook et al. 2010, nurses (abstract).** https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1001/archinternmed.2010.65&resultType=core&format=json . Result: 98 nurses, 4,271 administrations; each interruption raised procedural failures 12.1% and clinical errors 12.7%; estimated major error risk 2.3% with none and 4.7% with four; experience did not protect against clinical errors. Evidence: moderate to strong, observational.

Result: 36 workers; about 3 minutes per event; about 44% of interruptions self-initiated; 81.9% of interrupted work resumed the same day, in 23 min 15 s on average. Evidence: weak (interview).

## What this means for organising AI agent work

1. **A stated goal is not a shared goal.** Measured: 34% of responses matched intent after an unexpected change (32 episodes); 19% of intent statements gave the purpose. Air traffic relief briefings did not fix early-on-position errors. No remedy has been measured. Inference: check understanding by asking what the receiver would do when the plan breaks, then compare it with what the owner of the goal expected.
2. **A completed step is a claim.** Measured: 41% audited against near-100% self-report (Alberta); threefold hand-hygiene change under observation; adherence of 52-97% to double checks; announced laboratory tests outperform blind ones. Inference: derive "done" from artefacts and independently sampled evidence. Do not count boxes the worker ticked.
3. **Mandating a procedure and getting its effect are different things.** Measured: Haynes and Haugen gains against no effect in Ontario; the explanation is untested. Inference: a process step should exist because a named failure happens at that point, and its effect should be measured after it is introduced.
4. **Handover content matters; the act of handing over is less dangerous than it looks.** Measured: I-PASS bundle cut errors by 23% with no extra time; the randomised anaesthesia trial found no harm where observational data had suggested a large one. Inference: an agent stopping and another taking over can be routine, if the record passed on is structured and its reception checked. Beware observational claims about handover harm, including in your own logs, because hard work is what gets handed over.
5. **Load per coordinator may matter more than shift pattern.** Measured: ROSTERS' extra errors vanished after adjusting for patients per resident. Inference: treat the number of live items per supervising agent or person as a limit to design around.
6. **A second independent look finds more, at a cost, and one look finds little.** Measured: a blinded second reader added 13.3% cancers and 0.6 points of recall; a single expert found 2.58 of 9 seeded major errors; sight of the first opinion moved agreement and precision, with no clear detection loss at 1.1 million women. Double checking in medication is unproven. Inference: have reviewers reach a verdict before seeing the author's claims or another verdict. Do not expect one reviewer to be a gate. Reviewers of the same model may share blind spots, which none of these studies tested.
7. **A human approving AI output is a weak safety layer exactly when the AI is wrong.** Measured: radiologists dropped to 20-46% correct when the AI was wrong; automation bias resists training and instruction; combinations were on average worse than the better party in decision tasks, and they gained only when the human was better than the AI. Inference: reserve the human for what the human does better (goals, priorities, judgement on novel trade-offs). Do not use the owner as a line-by-line reviewer.
8. **The owner's attention is damaged by count and repetition, and by interruption.** Measured: 30% less acceptance per extra reminder per encounter, about 10% per 5 points of repeats, no effect of workload as such; overrides of 49-96%; a 2.8 s interruption doubled sequence errors. Inference: few asks, one per decision, never repeated, batched, each carrying what is needed to resume. Agents' resumption costs are a different matter and were not studied here.
9. **Rare catches decay, and the seeds used to test them can be spotted.** Measured: rare targets are missed; a burst of high prevalence with feedback shifted criterion in part; endoscopists' unaided detection fell after AI exposure; pilots' cognitive skills suffered more than their hands; models recognised agentic tests at AUC up to 0.95; blind cases yielded no errors but 40% inconclusive. Inference: use seeded faults drawn from real work and refresh them, treat seed scores as a ceiling, count "cannot say" as a result, and have the supervisor do some unaided work. Whether seeds raise real detection is unmeasured in what I could open.
10. **After a failure, prefer structural change to another rule.** Measured: the commonest actions from 302 root cause analyses were training and policy reinforcement, and events recurred. Inference: a fix that only tells workers to be more careful should count as no fix. Blame-free reporting is well designed in ASRS (independent custodian, immunity, deletion of identity), but its causal contribution is not measured, so copy the design without assuming the size of the gain.
