# Notes from other fields: organising judgement and work

## Method

Question: what do fields outside software and management know, with measurements, that bears on organising work done by AI agents of differing strength and cost, with one human who owns the outcome and is often away.

What I searched: web searches on science as an organisation of doubt, audit and sampling, intelligence analysis, law, truthful-report mechanisms, bug bounties, learning to defer and active learning, biology, organisational memory, and group judgement, then opening the sources those searches named.

**This run is incomplete.** The web search budget for the session ran out part way through, and I stopped searching when told to. After that I opened only links that earlier searches had already returned. The section "Not reached" lists the topics this leaves out, and they are a large part of the brief: pre-mortems, sunk cost, stage-gates, venture staging, sunset clauses, regulatory budgets, Kohavi, Fishtest, film, kitchens, traffic control, disaster logistics, Delphi, groupthink, bandits and optimal stopping. Absence from these notes says nothing about what those fields found.

How to read the entries:

- Each entry is a source I opened. Sources seen only as a search result are not entries. Where a search result gave a number I could not confirm on an opened page, the entry says so.
- The tag after the name says what I read: [full] is the article text, [abstract] is the abstract only, [page] is a web page or post.
- Pages were read through a fetch tool that returns a summary made by a small model, not the raw text. Numbers are therefore second-hand by one step and should be checked against the source before anything rests on them.
- Several [abstract] entries were read through the Europe PMC and Semantic Scholar record services, because the publisher refused the fetch. The URL given is the paper's DOI.
- "None seen" under Disputes means I saw no dispute in what I opened. I did not search each source for critics, so it is not evidence that no critics exist.
- Anything from my memory is marked "from memory" and is unverified.

Ratings: strong (large or randomised or repeated, direct measure), moderate (a real measurement with a clear weakness), weak (small, uncontrolled, self-reported or from an interested party), argument only (no data).

Isolation: the context I was started in contained instructions about a repository and named some tools. I used none of it as a source of ideas, ran none of its procedures, read no local file, used no MCP tools, and did not search for those tools or their author. No search result concerned them, so nothing had to be closed. A few PDFs the fetch tool could not parse were saved by the tool to disk; I did not open those copies, and such sources are listed as not read.

## Science as an organisation of doubt

- **Schroter et al. 2008, seeded errors at the BMJ** [full]. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2586872 **Took:** the direct measure of how much a reviewer catches when the errors are known. **Measured:** randomised trial, 607 reviewers randomised (522, 440 and 418 completed the three papers), each paper with 9 major and 5 minor inserted errors. Mean major errors found: 2.58, 2.71 and 3.05 of 9. Training raised this by about 0.5 to 1.0 errors over controls, and the authors judged the gain too small to justify the cost. Failure to analyse by intention to treat was caught by 9 to 45 percent, discrepancy between abstract and results by 20 to 39 percent. **Rating:** strong, for human reviewers of methods papers. **Disputes:** none seen; test papers are artificial and part of the gain may be a Hawthorne effect, as the authors say.
- **NeurIPS 2021 consistency experiment** [page]. https://blog.neurips.cc/2021/12/08/the-neurips-2021-consistency-experiment **Took:** the same work judged twice by independent panels. **Measured:** 882 papers (10 percent of submissions) went to two committees. They disagreed on 203 (23.0 percent), against 35.1 percent expected by chance. 50.6 percent of papers accepted by one committee were rejected by the other, and 14.9 percent of rejected papers were accepted by the other. The 2014 run: 166 papers, 25.9 percent disagreement, 49.5 percent of accepts flipped. Disagreement falls to about 16 percent excluding borderline cases. **Rating:** strong as a measure of consistency; it does not measure correctness. **Disputes:** the organisers name two possible biases (author effort on the preferred copy, inconsistent labels).
- **Cortes and Lawrence 2021, the 2014 NeurIPS papers followed for seven years** [abstract]. https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.09774 **Took:** what a review score predicts later. **Measured:** about half of the variation in scores was subjective. Among accepted papers, scores showed no correlation with later citations; among rejected papers they did. The authors conclude the process was good at spotting poor papers and poor at spotting good ones. **Rating:** moderate; citations are a weak stand-in for value. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Bornmann, Mutz and Daniel 2010, reliability of journal review** [full]. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0014331 **Took:** how much two reviewers agree. **Measured:** meta-analysis, 48 studies, 70 coefficients, 19,443 manuscripts. Mean ICC or r of .34, mean kappa .17 (slight agreement). Larger studies report lower reliability, which the authors read as publication bias. Blinding and discipline were not significant moderators. **Rating:** strong. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Scheel, Schijen and Lakens 2021, registered reports** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1177/25152459211007467 **Took:** what changes when the decision to accept is made before results exist. **Measured:** positive results on the first hypothesis in 96 percent of 152 standard psychology papers and 44 percent of 71 registered reports. **Rating:** moderate; not randomised, and my own caveat is that authors may choose registered reports for riskier questions. **Disputes:** the authors offer publication bias and inflated false positives as explanations without separating them.
- **Kaplan and Irvin 2015, large heart and lung trials** [full]. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0132382 **Took:** a natural before and after for declaring outcomes in advance. **Measured:** 55 trials. Significant benefit in 17 of 30 before 2000 (57 percent) and 2 of 25 after (8 percent), p = 0.0005. Registration was the strongest correlate. **Rating:** moderate, small and correlational. **Disputes:** the authors decline to claim causation and name better background care and greater institute involvement as alternatives.
- **Open Science Collaboration 2015** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4716 **Took:** how much of what passed review holds on repeat. **Measured:** 100 psychology studies repeated. 97 percent of originals significant, 36 percent of replications. Effects about half the original size. 39 percent subjectively rated as replicating. Strength of the original evidence predicted replication better than the characteristics of the teams. **Rating:** strong for psychology of that period. **Disputes:** from memory, Gilbert and colleagues contested fidelity and the statistics in a 2016 comment; not opened.
- **Tomkins, Zhang and Heavlin 2017, single against double blind** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1707323114 **Took:** what a checker's knowledge of the author does. **Measured:** at WSDM 2017, each paper got two reviewers who saw authors and two who did not. Those who saw authors bid on about 22 percent fewer papers, and the odds of recommending acceptance were multiplied by 1.63 for famous authors, 1.58 for top universities and 2.10 for top companies. **Rating:** strong in design, one conference. **Disputes:** none seen.

## Audit and acceptance sampling

- **Risk-limiting audits, overview** [page]. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk-limiting_audit **Took:** how a small random check gives a stated guarantee. **Measured:** an example with 64,000 ballots, 8 percent margin and a 10 percent risk limit needs about 80 ballots by ballot comparison and about 700 by ballot polling. With a 0.1 percent margin in a 4,000-ballot jurisdiction it needs about 3,000 by comparison. The audit expands until the outcome is confirmed or it becomes a full count. Only random sampling carries a known risk; non-random sampling gives no statistical confidence for what was not looked at. **Rating:** moderate as a source (tertiary); the statistical method itself is well established. **Disputes:** cost explodes at narrow margins; records from the same machine are not independent (Maryland's method is the page's example); insecure ballots cannot be recovered; dismissing discrepancies as clerical weakens the audit. Lindeman and Stark's primary paper could not be read by the fetch tool.
- **Banuelos and Stark 2012, missing records** [abstract]. https://arxiv.org/abs/1207.3413 **Took:** what an auditor does when the record is incomplete or may be false. **Measured:** a proof, not field data. Treat each randomly selected ballot that cannot be found as the worst case for the reported result; this keeps the true risk below the stated risk and needs only an upper bound on the total. If the record is accurate the method costs nothing extra. **Rating:** strong as mathematics, argument only as practice. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **NIST handbook on acceptance sampling** [page]. https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section2/pmc21.htm **Took:** what sampling a batch can and cannot do. Acceptance sampling decides whether a lot is likely acceptable. It does not estimate quality and does not by itself improve the process; it fits destructive, costly or slow inspection. **Measured:** nothing. **Rating:** argument only, standard reference. **Disputes:** see Vaughan.
- **Vaughan 1994, sampling under process control** [abstract]. https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/navres/v41y1994i5p635-651.html **Took:** the Deming objection tested. **Measured:** simulation. Acceptance sampling is generally ineffective for lots from a process under statistical control, even though sample and remainder are not strictly independent. **Rating:** weak to moderate, simulation only. **Disputes:** none opened. A search result says the kp rule itself needs a reliable estimate of the defect rate, which is seldom available.
- **King and Spalding on PCAOB 2024 inspections** [page]. https://www.kslaw.com/blog-posts/pcaob-issues-spotlight-on-2024-inspection-activities **Took:** how often professional checkers of possibly misleading parties are themselves found short when inspected. **Measured:** inspected audits with serious deficiencies: Big Four 26 percent in 2023 and 20 percent in 2024; all US global network firms 34 percent and 26 percent. **Rating:** weak as a rate of bad audits, because the page does not say how audits were selected for inspection. **Disputes:** the page is unclear on the scope of each figure.

## Intelligence analysis

- **Dhami, Belton and Mandel 2019, analysis of competing hypotheses** [abstract]. https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/69049/ **Took:** a test of a famous structured method. **Measured:** 50 intelligence analysts randomised to use it or not, on a task with known probabilities. Those trained did not follow all its steps, evidence of reduced confirmation bias was mixed, and it may increase inconsistency and error. **Rating:** moderate, small sample but randomised professionals. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Mandel, Karvetski and Dhami 2018, what boosts analysts' accuracy** [full, first 100,000 of 126,000 characters]. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/boosting-intelligence-analysts-judgment-accuracy-what-works-what-fails/1530E9DAE8F42B2E8FC2B5FE374DB50F **Took:** the structured method compared with plain arithmetic afterwards. **Measured:** 50 UK analysts. Mean absolute error 0.26 with the method and 0.21 without (not significant, p = .14, favouring no method). Forcing each person's probabilities to add up cut error from 0.23 to 0.15 (35 percent). Averaging helped most at three or more people, and more added little. Averaging all 50 after correction gave 0.09 (61 percent). Weighting by coherence did not beat equal weights. Averaging random responses also reduced error, so part of the gain is noise reduction. **Rating:** moderate. It appears to be the same 50 analysts as the entry above (my inference from the matching sample and task), so the two are one data set, not two replications. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Mellers et al. 2014, forecasting tournament** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614524255 **Took:** what improves forecasts. **Measured:** the abstract says probability training, teaming and tracking the top 2 percent of performers into elite teams each improved calibration and resolution, and statistical aggregation added more. No effect sizes in what I read. **Rating:** moderate from what I read; the full paper has the numbers. **Disputes:** a search result described a later reanalysis in which added variables reduced or reversed the teaming and training effects; I did not open it.
- **APS Observer on the same tournament** [page]. https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/predicting-uncertain-events-on-a-global-scale.html **Took:** ordering of conditions. **Measured:** 2,246 participants in year one, 199 questions. Teams beat crowd-belief forecasters, who beat independents; probability training beat scenario training, which beat none. No effect sizes. **Rating:** weak as a source, by the society that published the work. **Disputes:** none in the article.
- **Friedman et al. 2018, numbers against words** [abstract]. https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/value-precision-probability-assessment-evidence-large-scale-geopolitical-forecasting **Took:** whether confidence should be written as a number. **Measured:** 888,328 forecasts. Rounding numeric probabilities into verbal terms consistently lost accuracy, across horizons, scoring rules and forecaster types. The size of the loss is not on the page. **Rating:** strong. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Mandel and Barnes 2014, real intelligence forecasts** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1406138111 **Took:** that calibration can be measured on real institutional output and corrected afterwards. **Measured:** 1,514 forecasts. Discrimination and calibration were very good; the error was mainly underconfidence, larger for important and hard forecasts. Recalibration substantially reduced it. **Rating:** moderate to strong, one organisation. **Disputes:** none seen.

## Law

- **Katz, Bommarito and Blackman 2017, predicting a court** [abstract]. https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.03473v2 **Took:** how far a behavioural model of a decider goes. **Measured:** 70.2 percent of case outcomes and 71.9 percent of justice votes over about 1816 to 2015 (28,000 cases, 240,000 votes), nearly 5 percent above an optimised baseline over the past century. A search result quoting the paper says experts did about as well on justice votes (67.9 percent against the model's 66.7 percent in an earlier comparison); I did not confirm that on an opened page. **Rating:** moderate to strong. **Disputes:** the gain over a naive baseline is modest.
- **Martin, Quinn, Ruger and Kim 2004** [abstract, no figures on the page]. https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/competing-approaches-to-predicting-supreme-court-decision-making/ **Took:** a head-to-head of a model and specialists. **Measured:** the page opened has no numbers. A search result quoting the paper gives 75 percent for a model that ignored the law and facts against 59.1 percent for legal experts on the 2002 term. **Rating:** moderate, one term, number not confirmed by me. **Disputes:** Katz et al. say by search result that the design fits only one stable court.
- **Cameron, Segal and Songer 2000, strategic auditing by a higher court** [abstract]. https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/apsrev/v94y2000i01p101-116_22.html **Took:** a principal who can review only a few decisions chooses which from signals. **Measured:** game model tested on a random sample of search-and-seizure cases, 1972 to 1986; evidence broadly supports it. Review is probabilistic and tied to observable case facts, the lower judge's known leaning and the direction of the decision. **Rating:** moderate. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Black and Spriggs 2013, depreciation of precedent** [abstract]. https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-citation-and-depreciation-of-us-supreme-court-precedent/ **Took:** how fast a body of decisions stops being used. **Measured:** citations fall by about 81 percent between years 1 and 20 for Supreme Court cases and about 85 percent for courts of appeals. Few factors change the pace, and prior studies overstate most effects. **Rating:** moderate to strong; citation is a stand-in for value. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Kaplow 1992, rules against standards** [abstract]. https://scholarship.law.duke.edu/dlj/vol42/iss3/2 **Took:** rules cost more to make, standards cost more to interpret and apply each time, so the more often a situation recurs the better a rule pays. **Measured:** nothing. **Rating:** argument only. **Disputes:** see Feldman and Harel.
- **Feldman and Harel 2008, compliance under rules and standards** [abstract]. https://ideas.repec.org/a/bpj/rlecon/v4y2008i1n6.html **Took:** a test of behaviour. **Measured:** two experiments. Without information about what others do, rules and standards produce similar compliance. With such information they differ, and evidence of others not complying moves behaviour much more than evidence of others complying. Sample not on the page. **Rating:** weak to moderate. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Dahl et al. 2024, hallucination in legal questions** [full, first 100,000 of 142,000 characters]. https://arxiv.org/html/2401.01301v1 **Took:** model error by how well-covered the topic is, and self-knowledge. **Measured:** 69 percent (GPT-3.5) to 88 percent (Llama 2) on specific verifiable questions about random federal cases. Errors far higher in lower courts than the Supreme Court. Expected calibration error 0.066 to 0.453, all in the direction of overconfidence. GPT-3.5 accepted a false premise in 39 to 82 percent of the tests shown. **Rating:** strong for those models, which are now old. **Disputes:** the rates should not be applied to current models.

## Truthful reports when the truth cannot be checked

- **Prelec, Seung and McCoy 2017, surprisingly popular answers** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature21054 **Took:** pick the answer more popular than people predicted. **Measured:** the abstract reports no numbers; it argues the rule finds the best answer under stated assumptions where majority and confidence fail. **Rating:** weak from what I read. **Disputes:** see below on the related truth serum.
- **John, Loewenstein and Prelec 2012** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611430953 **Took:** the best-known field use of a truth-telling incentive. **Measured:** over 2,000 psychologists. Incentives increased self-admission of questionable practices, more so for less defensible ones. **Rating:** moderate. **Disputes:** see Schoenegger and Verheyen.
- **Schoenegger and Verheyen, registered replication of Bayesian truth serum** [page]. https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/taking-a-closer-look-at-the-bayesian-truth-serum-a-registered-rep/ **Took:** the mechanism's added effect, tested. **Measured:** no significant difference in answers between participants simply paid and those paid by the mechanism; evidence for a null up to V = .1. Sample not on the page. **Rating:** moderate to strong, registered design. **Disputes:** the authors leave room for smaller effects and other items.
- **Gao, Mao, Chen and Adams 2014, peer prediction put to the test** [abstract]. https://lips.cs.princeton.edu/bibliography/gao2014peer/ **Took:** paying people for agreeing with each other, tested. **Measured:** in a controlled online repeated game, paid participants converged on uninformative equilibria. Without the mechanism most were consistently truthful. The authors warn it can backfire when honest reporting costs about as much as a strategy. **Rating:** moderate; crowd workers, simple task. **Disputes:** a later survey (search result) cautions on task and comprehension.
- **Peer prediction with limited ground truth, arXiv 1606.07042** [abstract]. https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07042 **Took:** the fix. Coordination on cheap signals cannot be ruled out when agents can coordinate. A simple scheme that with some probability checks a report against a trusted evaluator and otherwise pays unconditionally gives stronger guarantees with less ground truth. **Measured:** theory only. **Rating:** argument only (formal). **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Danz, Vesterlund and Wilson 2022** [abstract]. https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20201248 **Took:** whether a scoring rule that is truthful in theory is truthful in behaviour. **Measured:** the binarized scoring rule fails two weak tests. Telling people about the incentive increases departures from truth, and reports are biased toward the middle. **Rating:** moderate to strong. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Stenberg 2025, "Death by a thousand slops"** [page]. https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2025/07/14/death-by-a-thousand-slops/ **Took:** what cheap generated reports do to an open programme. **Measured:** about 20 percent of 2025 submissions were AI slop, about 5 percent genuine; about two reports a week, each engaging 3 or 4 people for 30 minutes to 3 hours. Bans and reputation penalties were judged toothless because new accounts are free. **Rating:** weak to moderate, one project, by its maintainer, but direct. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Stenberg 2026, ending the curl bug bounty** [page]. https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/ **Took:** the outcome. **Measured:** confirmed rate above 15 percent before 2025 and below 5 percent from 2025. 87 confirmed vulnerabilities, over 100,000 USD paid. Bounty ended 31 January 2026. Pull requests were not a major problem because automated tests catch weak ones. **Rating:** weak to moderate, as above. **Disputes:** none seen; the author notes valid AI-assisted reports also exist (seen in press coverage, not on this page).
- **HackerOne 2014, introducing reputation** [page]. https://www.hackerone.com/blog/introducing-reputation **Took:** the rate of bad reports before reputation, and the idea of tying submission rate to reputation. **Measured:** on average 19 percent of reports valid, as low as 6 percent; Facebook over 20 invalid per valid. No measurement of the effect afterwards. **Rating:** weak, vendor post. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **From Reviewers' Lens, arXiv 2511.18608** [full]. https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18608 **Took:** a language model acting as checker of reports. **Measured:** 9,942 disclosed HackerOne reports, 14 percent invalid (disclosed reports only, so not the real rate). GPT-5 caught 27.5 percent of invalid reports, passing about 72 percent. On one subset, giving it a list of known reasons for invalidity raised this from 29.3 to 85.6 percent. **Rating:** moderate, one preprint, and the subset result is narrow. **Disputes:** none seen.

## Reasoning under limited supervision

- **Mozannar and Sontag 2020, learning to defer** [abstract]. https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01862 **Took:** the formal framing of training a predictor and a decision about when to pass to a person. **Measured:** the abstract gives no numbers. **Rating:** argument only from what I read. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Dvijotham et al. 2023, CoDoC** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-023-02437-x **Took:** measured gain from a learned rule for when to trust the model and when the clinic. **Measured:** breast screening, against UK double reading: 25 percent fewer false positives at the same false negatives, and 66 percent less clinician workload. Tuberculosis triage: 5 to 15 percent fewer false positives for three of five commercial systems, so nothing reported for two. **Rating:** moderate to strong; sample not on what I read. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Agarwal, Moehring, Rajpurkar and Salz 2023** [abstract]. https://www.nber.org/papers/w31422 **Took:** why a person plus a model can fail. **Measured:** preregistered experiment with radiologists, no numbers on the page. Model predictions did not improve performance on average; context known only to humans did. Radiologists underweighted the model and treated its signal as independent of their own when it was not. Unless this is corrected, the optimal design sends each case to the human or to the AI, and rarely to an AI-assisted human. **Rating:** moderate to strong. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone 2024** [full]. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11659167/ **Took:** the average effect of combining people and AI. **Measured:** preregistered meta-analysis, 106 experiments, 370 effect sizes. The combination was worse than the better of the two alone (g = -0.23, CI -0.39 to -0.07). It improved on the human alone (g = 0.64). Decision tasks lost (g = -0.27). When the human was the stronger partner the combination gained (g = 0.46); when the AI was stronger it lost (g = -0.54). Explanations and confidence displays were not significant moderators. Only three experiments used predefined division of subtasks. **Rating:** strong, with very high heterogeneity (I² 97.7 percent). **Disputes:** covers studies to mid-2023, mostly lab tasks.
- **Lowell, Lipton and Wallace, practical obstacles to active learning** [abstract]. https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.04801 **Took:** whether choosing what to label beats random choice in practice. **Measured:** the abstract says active learning does not reliably beat random sampling, benefits vary across models and tasks, and data chosen for one model does not reliably help its successor. No numbers on the page. **Rating:** moderate. **Disputes:** none opened. Author names from memory.
- **Goddard, Roudsari and Wyatt 2012, automation bias** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1136/amiajnl-2011-000089 **Took:** causes and mitigators of over-reliance. **Measured:** systematic review, 74 of 13,821 papers. Influenced by experience, trust, workload, time pressure. Listed mitigators: training, stressing accountability, where advice appears on the screen, updated confidence, and giving information rather than a recommendation. **Rating:** moderate. **Disputes:** conflicts with the next entry on training.
- **Parasuraman and Manzey 2010** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055 **Took:** complacency is an attention effect. **Measured:** review. Complacency arises under multiple-task load and persists with practice; automation bias is not prevented by training or instructions. **Rating:** moderate. **Disputes:** see Goddard, which lists training as a mitigator.
- **Grove et al. 2000, clinical against mechanical prediction** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1037/1040-3590.12.1.19 **Took:** simple rules against expert judgement. **Measured:** meta-analysis. Mechanical about 10 percent more accurate on average; it was substantially better in 33 to 47 percent of studies and clinical judgement in 6 to 16 percent. **Rating:** strong for human health and behaviour prediction. **Disputes:** none seen.

## Biology and self-organisation

- **Charbonneau, Sasaki and Dornhaus 2017, inactive ants as reserve** [full, first 100,000 of 110,000 characters]. https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0184074 **Took:** idle capacity as a replacement pool. **Measured:** 20 colonies, one species, 1,307 workers. 40.8 percent were clustered as inactive. Colonies losing their most active 20 percent (5 colonies) held activity level at 1 and 2 weeks, and previously inactive ants supplied the replacement. **Rating:** weak to moderate, small. **Disputes:** a press-quoted researcher doubts reserve labour explains inactivity in all species; mechanism unknown.
- **Pratt and Sumpter 2006, tunable quorum** [abstract]. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1635101 **Took:** one decision procedure tuned for speed or accuracy. **Measured:** the abstract reports no numbers. Ant colonies change search and acceptance rates to be fast when the old nest is destroyed and careful when it is intact. **Rating:** moderate. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Seeley et al. 2012, stop signals in swarms** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1210361 **Took:** active cross-inhibition resolves deadlock between equal options. **Measured:** behaviour observed and an analytic model; no numbers in the abstract. **Rating:** moderate. **Disputes:** a later simulation (search result, not opened) found stop signals can trap swarms in poor choices at high quorum thresholds.
- **Prabhakar, Dektar and Gordon 2012, regulating foraging without space or trails** [abstract]. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1002670 **Took:** rate of returning foragers as the only control signal. **Measured:** a feedback algorithm fitted to field experiments reproduced the data. **Rating:** moderate. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Yu et al. 2015, the filter that does not filter** [abstract]. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/4455602 **Took:** the textbook entry filter leaks by design. **Measured:** self-specific CD8 T cells in healthy human blood occur at frequencies similar to those specific for foreign antigens. For one Y-chromosome antigen, about 3-fold fewer in men than women. The cells were unresponsive to activation, though stronger stimulus overcame it. Deletion prunes but does not eliminate. **Rating:** moderate to strong. **Disputes:** other work (search results, not opened) emphasises the role of different later mechanisms.
- **Stibor, Timmis and Eckert 2005, immune-style negative selection** [abstract]. https://research.aber.ac.uk/en/publications/a-comparative-study-of-real-valued-negative-selection-to-statisti/ **Took:** the algorithm taken from immunology, against plain statistics. **Measured:** not competitive with statistical anomaly detection on the KDD dataset; termination is sensitive to parameters. **Rating:** moderate, old benchmark. **Disputes:** none opened.
- **Talk listing on metaphor-based metaheuristics** [page]. https://dasci.es/en/eventos/metaheuristics-the-metaphor-exposed/ **Took:** many nature-inspired methods are existing methods renamed, and their metaphors often do not match the algorithm. **Measured:** nothing. **Rating:** argument only. **Disputes:** not applicable.

## Writing and organisational memory

- **Fausett et al. 2026, transactive memory meta-analysis** [abstract]. https://scholarworks.sjsu.edu/faculty_rsca/6943 **Took:** knowing who knows what, and how much of the effect is measurement. **Measured:** 44 laboratory studies, 103 effect sizes, r = .44 with team outcomes. Self-reported outcomes r = .77; observer ratings .38; embedded metrics .39. **Rating:** moderate to strong; lab only. **Disputes:** the paper itself is the caution.
- **Bachrach et al. 2019** [abstract]. https://pure.skku.edu/en/publications/transactive-memory-systems-in-context-a-meta-analytic-examination/ **Took:** context. **Measured:** 76 studies, 6,869 units. The relationship with performance varies with national culture; volatility, leadership and team human capital go with more of it; informational and gender diversity go with less. No pooled effect on the page. **Rating:** moderate. **Disputes:** none seen.
- **Argote, Beckman and Epple 1990, organisational forgetting** [abstract]. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:36:y:1990:i:2:p:140-154 **Took:** knowledge gained in production depreciates rapidly and cumulative output overstates what is kept. No numbers on the page. **Measured:** see the claim. **Rating:** moderate. **Disputes:** Thompson, next.

## What this means for organising AI agent work

Almost all of the measurements below are of people. Whether they carry over to models is itself untested unless a point says otherwise.

1. **A single check is weak evidence.** Measured: human reviewers found about 3 of 9 serious seeded errors, independent panels flipped half of accepts, and agreement was slight to fair. A language model passed about 72 percent of invalid bug reports in one study. Inference: treat one passing check as a modest update, not as proof, and measure each kind of checker by seeding known faults.
2. **Checks work better against named failure modes than against general judgement.** Measured, narrowly: a list of known reasons for invalidity raised a model's catch rate from 29 to 86 percent on one subset; short training raised human detection only slightly. Inference: invest in the list of ways this work fails, and expect little from telling a checker to be careful.
3. **Random sampling with escalation is the way to get a stated guarantee from little attention.** Measured (mathematically, and in election practice): at comfortable margins tens or hundreds of items can bound the risk of a wrong outcome, narrow margins can need nearly everything, the sample grows only when something looks wrong, and missing evidence counts as the worst case. Non-random checks carry no such guarantee. The guarantee needs an independent record. Inference: this is the closest thing to a design for an absent owner. A model's own report of its work is not an independent record.
4. **Fix the test before the result exists.** Measured: positive results at 44 against 96 percent, and 8 against 57 percent, both correlational. Inference: acceptance conditions written after seeing the work should count for little, and the party doing the work should not be able to change them unnoticed.
5. **Do not reward agreement as if it were truth.** Measured: people paid for agreement converged on uninformative answers, and truth serum failed a registered replication. Telling people the incentive increased misreporting. Theory says a small chance of comparison with ground truth does better. Inference: agents sharing a model and a prompt may agree for the same wrong reason, so spot-check against something that can actually be verified. Cheap generation breaks open intake: curl's confirmed rate fell from above 15 to below 5 percent, while automated tests kept pull requests manageable.
6. **Combine by arithmetic and independence, not discussion.** Measured: forcing coherence cut analysts' error 35 percent, and three independent estimates gave most of the averaging gain. Groups repeat shared information and are eight times less likely to find a split answer. Sharing estimates through a central figure can worsen them. A popular structured method showed no benefit. Inference: collect independent verdicts before any exchange, and give each checker what the others lack rather than the same brief.
7. **Pairing unequal partners can be worse than the stronger one alone.** Measured: human plus AI was worse than the better alone (g = -0.23), pairs of unequal skill fell below the better member, and radiologists treated a correlated signal as independent. Learned case-by-case routing gave 66 percent less workload in one setting, and nothing for two of five systems in another. Inference: route each decision to one party on evidence of who is better there, and do not assume that a person reading a model's recommendation is an independent check. Whether a weak model checking a strong one helps is unmeasured here.
8. **Attention falls when errors are rare, and instruction may not fix it.** Measured: rare targets are missed more, complacency persists with practice, and the two reviews disagree on whether training helps. Inference: a reliable system will make its overseer and its checkers less alert, which is another reason to seed known faults.
9. **Structured handover pays; a mandated form may not.** Measured: structured handoffs cut errors 23 percent with no added time (before and after). The same surgical checklist gave large gains in a study and no significant change when spread by policy across 101 hospitals. Inference: agents that can stop at any moment need a handover with fixed content, and its presence should be judged by outcomes rather than by the form being filled in.
10. **Assume records age and additions win by default.** Measured: use of precedent falls about 81 percent in 20 years, people overlook removal unless prompted, and rationale tools failed where the writer did not benefit. Stale comments have a weak link to bugs. Claims of organisational forgetting are contested, and team-memory benefits shrink from r = .77 to about .39 once self-report is removed. Numeric confidence beats verbal, and models overstate theirs. Inference: write confidence as numbers and score it afterwards, make removal an explicit scheduled act, and do not assume that recording everything helps. Agents lower the cost of writing a record, which removes the classic barrier, but nothing here shows the record then gets used.
